## Why is the Kyotango gut microbiome unusual?

Kyotango City, Kyoto Prefecture, has roughly three times the national centenarian rate. Its colorectal cancer incidence is about half that of Kyoto City. The Kyotango Longevity Cohort Study of the Kyoto Prefectural University of Medicine (798 participants aged 65+) has repeatedly found higher gut microbiota α-diversity in Kyotango residents than in non-Kyotango controls. **Butyrate-producing bacteria** such as *Roseburia*, *Coprococcus* and *Lachnospira* are particularly elevated.

Butyrate is a short-chain fatty acid that colonic bacteria produce by fermenting dietary fiber. It fuels the cells lining the colon, maintains the gut barrier and induces regulatory T cells that dampen chronic inflammation.

Butyrate producers depend on a steady supply of dietary fiber and fermented foods. The Kyotango gut environment may therefore reflect not only individual habits but a food culture carried across generations. This study asks **why Kyotango residents have this microbiome** by tracing their food culture from the Jomon period to the present.

The paper states plainly that it does not make causal claims. It is a **hypothesis-generating study**.

## The analytical procedure

<figure>
  <img src="/column-images/jef-kyotango-butyrate/method-flow-1600.webp" alt="Analytical procedure" />
  <figcaption><strong>Figure 1.</strong> Overview of the analytical procedure (from the paper). Five source types are processed with an LLM, verified against the original texts, cross-referenced with butyrate-producer substrates and compared across six eras.</figcaption>
</figure>

The sources come from five categories, each with its own discipline and conventions.

| Era | Source | Type |
|---|---|---|
| Jomon | Excavation report on the Hamazume shell midden (Kyotango City Board of Education) | Primary archaeological source |
| Yayoi–Kofun | Fish bones and human-bone isotopes from the Aoya-Kamijichi site (Tottori City) | Proxy data |
| Nara–Heian | Engishiki, Chapter 24 (927 CE) | Legal code |
| Medieval–Early Modern | Analysis of the Kebukigusa; mackerel food culture survey by the Tango Museum (questionnaire n = 379) | Food culture documents |
| Modern | Two papers from the Kyotango Longevity Cohort | Medical literature |

No quantitative faunal or floral report from a Yayoi–Kofun site in the Tango region could be found. The study therefore uses the Aoya-Kamijichi site in Tottori, which belongs to the same Sea of Japan coastal Yayoi culture and has been analyzed in detail. The paper flags this substitution as a limitation throughout.

The text of each source was fed to an LLM (Claude Sonnet), which extracted (a) food ingredient names, (b) descriptions of processing and preservation and (c) all quantitative data. **Every extracted data point was then checked against the original source, and anything that could not be confirmed was discarded.** The ingredients were organized by nutritional category and cross-referenced with the substrates of butyrate producers: soluble fiber, n-3 fatty acids and fermented foods.

## Experiment 1: What was eaten at the Hamazume shell midden?

<p class="experiment-label">Experiment 1</p>

<div class="setup">

**Setup**

- Source: the sixth excavation of the Hamazume shell midden, the only Jomon shell midden in Kyoto Prefecture; vertebrate and molluscan analyses
- Wet-sieving was used throughout, so fine fish bones and seeds were recovered
- Measures: number of identified specimens (NISP) for fish, minimum number of individuals (MNI) for shellfish

</div>

The sixth season recovered 1,785 vertebrate specimens, 93% of them fish (1,592). Among the 1,472 specimens from the 2 mm sieve fraction, three taxa dominated.

<figure>
  <img src="/column-images/jef-kyotango-butyrate/fish-1600.webp" alt="Fish remains at Hamazume" />
  <figcaption><strong>Figure 3.</strong> Fish remains from the Hamazume shell midden (≥2 mm fraction, N = 1,472). Flying fish, pufferfish and Clupeidae (mainly Japanese sardine) dominate.</figcaption>
</figure>

The flying fish are the telling detail. Only abdominal and caudal vertebrae were found, and cranial bones are absent. The original authors interpret this as heads and viscera being removed before storage. Drying or salting may thus have existed in the Jomon period. Preserving seasonal migratory fish for year-round use looks like a precursor of later practices such as heshiko and mejiri (dried fish).

The shellfish include at least 26 species from five habitats: brackish lagoon, inner bay, open-sea rocky substrate, open-sea sandy substrate and freshwater.

<figure>
  <img src="/column-images/jef-kyotango-butyrate/shellfish-1600.webp" alt="Shellfish at Hamazume" />
  <figcaption><strong>Figure 4.</strong> Top medium-to-large shellfish at Hamazume (MNI), colored by habitat. The Pacific oyster is reported as "more than 100", so it is drawn hatched at 100.</figcaption>
</figure>

Acorns were also abundant. They are rich in indigestible starch that colonic bacteria ferment into short-chain fatty acids including butyrate. Fiber sources were on the menu before fermented foods appeared.

## Experiment 2: Foods on record from Yayoi to the Medieval period

<p class="experiment-label">Experiment 2</p>

**Yayoi–Kofun (proxy).** At Aoya-Kamijichi, black sea bream had the highest MNI (64), followed by bluefin tuna longer than 1 m, red sea bream, mackerel, pufferfish, sea bass, mullet and crucian carp from paddy fields. Collagen isotopes of 18 human skeletons give δ13C of −19.7 to −18.0‰ (mean −19.1‰), so C3 resources such as rice were the main carbon source. δ15N, however, is higher than a purely terrestrial diet would predict. The original mixing model estimates a marine contribution of 15–35% (mean about 22%).

**Nara–Heian.** Extracting every food item from the Tango Province section of the Engishiki (927 CE) gives eight entries.

| Item | Modern identification | Relevance to gut microbiota |
|---|---|---|
| Boiled-salted ayu | Salt-cured sweetfish | n-3 fatty acids; salt-brining preservation |
| Fresh salmon (×3/year) | Salmon | EPA/DHA |
| Sesame oil | Sesame oil | Linoleic and oleic acid |
| Konjac | *Amorphophallus konjac* | Glucomannan (soluble fiber) |
| Japanese pepper (sansho) | *Zanthoxylum piperitum* | Same ingredient as in modern heshiko |
| Japanese bitter orange | *Citrus tachibana* | Fiber, vitamin C |
| Chestnut | *Castanea crenata* | Indigestible starch |
| Seaweed | Wakame, etc. | Fucoidan, alginate (soluble fiber) |

Sansho is the standout. Recorded as a Tango tribute item in 927 CE, it is still an essential seasoning in heshiko, giving **a documented continuity of at least about 1,100 years**. Jomon acorns and Heian chestnuts and konjac also play the same role: both supply hard-to-digest carbohydrates.

**Medieval.** The Kebukigusa (early Edo period) lists Tango specialties including Ine-ura yellowtail, sardine, sea cucumber, mejiri (dried fish), Kumihama miru seaweed, nori, a clam from near Amanohashidate and sesame. Miru and nori are both soluble fiber sources. Mejiri continues the same head-removal preservation seen in the Jomon flying fish.

## Experiment 3: Heshiko and the modern cohort

<p class="experiment-label">Experiment 3</p>

**Early Modern–Modern.** Heshiko is mackerel that is first salted, then packed with rice bran, salt and chili pepper (with sansho) and fermented for a full year through the summer. Nutritionally, it combines all three pillars in one food:

- Rice bran: soluble fiber, a direct substrate for butyrate producers
- Year-long fermentation: lactic acid bacteria make lactate, which *Anaerostipes* converts to butyrate (cross-feeding)
- Mackerel: EPA and DHA

In the Tango Museum survey (n = 379), Kyotango residents most often named Tango barazushi as a "familiar" food (30%), followed by heshiko (13.6%).

**Modern.** Naito et al. found that elderly Kyotango residents (frailty analysis n = 798) had higher gut microbiota α-diversity than 599 non-Kyotango controls, and that soluble fiber intake was significantly lower in the frail group. Scores on the rJDI12, a measure of adherence to the traditional Japanese diet, were significantly higher in the non-frail group (p = 0.033), and higher rJDI12 was associated with more *Faecalibacterium*.

## Experiment 4: Butyrate production potential by era (BPPS)

<p class="experiment-label">Experiment 4</p>

<div class="setup">

**Setup**

- BPPS: four axes (A soluble fiber, B fermented and preserved foods, C seafood diversity, D overall dietary diversity), each scored on six levels from 0 to 2.5; total 10 points
- Each level is defined by a written rubric (e.g. C = 2.5 requires ten or more taxa from four or more habitats with NISP or MNI counts)
- Raters: the first author, plus Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct, Gemma-3-12B-it and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct run locally with greedy decoding
- The final score of each cell is the median of the four raters; the rubric and the evidence summaries were frozen before any rating

</div>

<figure>
  <img src="/column-images/jef-kyotango-butyrate/bpps-era-1600.webp" alt="BPPS by era" />
  <figcaption><strong>Figure 5.</strong> BPPS by era (median of four raters). Stacks show the four axes; whiskers show the lowest and highest rater total. Yayoi–Kofun (hatched) rests on proxy data.</figcaption>
</figure>

Totals range from 4.25 (Yayoi–Kofun) to 7.5 (Modern), and every era from Nara–Heian onward falls between 6.5 and 7.5. The paper, however, reads **the axis profiles, not small differences in totals,** as the main result, because each source type records a different range of foods.

<figure>
  <img src="/column-images/jef-kyotango-butyrate/axis-profile-1600.webp" alt="Axis profile by era" />
  <figcaption><strong>Figure 6.</strong> Scores on the four axes by era (median of four raters). Darker means higher.</figcaption>
</figure>

The axis profiles show how the three pillars developed.

- **Seafood diversity (C)** peaks in the Jomon period (2.25), which has specimen counts for three fish taxa and 26 shellfish species from five habitats.
- **Fermented and preserved foods (B)** rise over time: indirect preservation traces in the Jomon period (0.5), preserved tribute items in Nara–Heian (1.0), fermented soy foods in the Medieval period (1.5) and heshiko in the Early Modern period (2.25).
- **The Early Modern period** reaches the maximum on fiber (A = 2.5) but scores only 1.0 on C and D, because its source is a survey of mackerel food culture that records few other foods.

The Yayoi–Kofun total of 4.25 is the least certain, since it rests entirely on proxy data.

## Experiment 5: How well did the raters agree?

<p class="experiment-label">Experiment 5</p>

A score like the BPPS is only useful if it does not depend on who does the scoring. Four raters therefore scored all 24 cells (six eras × four axes) independently with the written rubric, and their agreement was measured.

| Rater set | ICC(2,1) | ICC(2,k) | α | Exact | Within 0.5 pt | MAD |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| All four raters | 0.72 | 0.91 | 0.71 | 47% | 82% | 0.36 |
| Three LLM raters | 0.76 | 0.90 | 0.75 | 46% | 85% | 0.35 |
| Human vs. LLM median | 0.65 | – | – | 46% | 79% | – |

By common guidelines, this is moderate-to-good reliability for a single rater and excellent reliability for the pooled score. The human rater matched the LLM median exactly as often (46%) as the LLMs matched each other.

<figure>
  <img src="/column-images/jef-kyotango-butyrate/agreement-1600.webp" alt="Agreement by axis" />
  <figcaption><strong>Figure 7.</strong> Agreement between rater pairs by axis (four raters, 24 cells). Blue is exact agreement; the full bar is agreement within 0.5 points.</figcaption>
</figure>

Agreement was highest for fermented foods (B), where the level follows directly from whether a source names a fermentation process. Disagreement concentrated on A and D, which require counting food categories or groups from narrative text, and it was mostly a 0.5-point difference.

The main pattern does not depend on equal weighting. Under eight alternative weightings (dropping each axis, or doubling each axis), Yayoi–Kofun stayed lowest and all eras from Nara–Heian onward stayed between 5.7 and 8.0. The Modern era ranked first under equal weights and six of the alternatives. The Early Modern period ranked first only when overall dietary diversity (D) was removed, which confirms that its position depends on the narrow scope of its source.

## What we know and what we don't

The study finds three things.

- Across six independent source types, **diverse seafood and dietary fiber sources** have stayed in Kyotango's food culture from the Jomon period to today, and **preserved and fermented foods** were added over time until heshiko combined all three.
- Rubric-based scoring by four raters reproduces this pattern with excellent pooled reliability (ICC(2,k) = 0.91), and the pattern survives alternative axis weights.
- Sansho from the Engishiki (927 CE) is still used in heshiko, showing about 1,100 years of continuity at the level of a single ingredient.

The limitations are equally clear.

- The evidence is circumstantial: food culture aligns structurally with the substrates of butyrate producers. **It is not a causal demonstration**, and thousands of years separate the archaeological and microbiome data.
- There are no Tango data for the Yayoi–Kofun period; a Tottori site stands in.
- Shell midden data are biased toward durable remains such as bone and shell.
- Assuming that gut microbiota are passed down over thousands of years goes beyond current scientific support.
- Genetics, exercise, sleep, social ties, climate and other factors cannot be ruled out.
- The rubric thresholds and equal weights were set by the authors. Three raters were LLMs, whose judgments are not ground truth, and the human rater is an author. Scoring by independent experts in archaeology and nutrition is still needed.

Future work includes systematic faunal and floral analysis at Yayoi–Kofun sites in Kyotango, stable isotope and ancient DNA analysis of any recovered skeletons, and the same diachronic analysis for other longevity regions such as Okinawa and the Mediterranean.

The rubric, the evidence summary for each era, all ratings with their justifications, and the code for LLM scoring and agreement analysis are provided as Additional file 1 of the paper.
