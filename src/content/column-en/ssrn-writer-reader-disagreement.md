## When writers and readers disagree

The emotion a writer felt and the emotion readers perceive in the same text do not always match. From the perspectivist view, which treats annotator variation as information rather than noise, an emotion recognizer should be judged not only by its fit to one gold label but also by whether it reproduces **how the perspectives differ**. Several corpora record both the writer's and the readers' judgments on the same texts.

At the same time, more and more of the training data for emotion recognizers is generated by large language models. In that setting, whether an example describes the writer's or the reader's emotion is set only by a prompt, not by the judgments of people. It is not clear what a few added human labels correct.

Evaluation is also tricky. A single aggregate measure of perspective mixes the average tendency of each perspective with the differences on individual texts. A predictor that ignores the text and returns each perspective's label distribution already behaves differently for the two perspectives, so the aggregate measure can be large. We therefore built a framework that **measures the average difference separately from the direction and size of text-level differences**, and asked which information reproduces each.

## A three-level evaluation framework

<figure>
  <img src="/column-images/ssrn-writer-reader-disagreement/framework-1600.webp" alt="Three-level evaluation framework" />
  <figcaption><strong>Figure 1.</strong> Overview of the framework (Figure 1 of the paper). (a) Texts are generated under a perspective instruction and a student is trained on them. (b) The student is adapted with k human labels under three information conditions: paired anchors, shuffled anchors, and the label distribution only. (c) Pairs of recognizers are evaluated by predictive loss, the population-level difference, and the text-level difference.</figcaption>
</figure>

The framework has three stages.

1. **Perspective-conditioned generation.** An LLM receives a label specification and a perspective instruction. The writer instruction asks it to write as the person who feels the emotions. The reader instruction asks for a text that makes readers feel them (elicitation) or a text from which readers would infer that the writer feels them (attribution). Label specifications are shared across perspectives, so only the instruction differs. A leakage filter fixed in advance removes texts that name the emotion, and a student is trained on the rest. Because it has seen no human labels, we call it the k = 0 student.
2. **Few-label adaptation.** The student is adapted with k human-labelled texts (anchors), once with writer labels and once with reader labels, which gives a writer–reader pair. The methods are calibration (a temperature and per-class biases on the frozen student), fine-tuning on the anchors alone, and fine-tuning from the k = 0 student. Label-only controls remove the pairing: shuffled labels, a prior-ratio adjustment, and a marginal-constant predictor that outputs the anchors' label distribution for every text. Whatever these controls reach comes from the label set rather than from the pairing of texts and labels.
3. **Three-level evaluation.** We measure the predictive loss (NLL) of each model, the population-level difference, and the text-level difference.

The population-level shift D compares how far each recognizer in the pair is from the writer and reader label distributions, in KL divergence. It is reported relative to the gap G of reference models trained on all labels of the same texts. The paper shows that **for a predictor that ignores the text, D equals the symmetrized KL divergence between the two label distributions**. If the perspectives differ in label frequencies, D is positive without reading a single text.

The text-level difference is measured on the human difference Δ(x) and the predicted difference after their means are removed.

| Measure | Meaning |
|---|---|
| A (centered alignment) | Direction of the text-level differences. Cosine similarity between predicted and human differences: 1 = same direction, 0 = unrelated |
| M (magnitude ratio) | Size of the predicted differences relative to the human differences |
| R²_text | Share of text-level variance explained in squared error |

The three are linked by R²_text = 2AM − M² = A² − (M − A)². Explained variance cannot exceed A², and enlarging the predicted differences helps only up to M = A.

## Setup

<div class="setup">

**Setup**

- Data: WRIME v2 (35,000 Japanese social-media posts; the writer's own intensity ratings of 8 emotions on a 0–3 scale, plus ratings from three readers) and crowd-enVent (English event descriptions; the writer chose one of 13 classes, and 1,200 texts also have labels from five readers)
- crowd-enVent split: the 1,200 reader-labelled texts were split into a reader pool of 397 and a test set of 803; the other 5,400 texts form a writer pool
- Generators: GPT-4o-mini and Qwen3-8B (temperature 1.0, three paraphrases per instruction); 5,148–5,880 texts kept per generator and perspective after filtering
- Students: a Japanese BERT for WRIME and RoBERTa-base for crowd-enVent
- Anchor sizes k: 5 / 20 / 100 / 500 on WRIME and 5 / 20 / 100 / 397 on crowd-enVent, with 10–50 random draws per size
- Intervals: 95% intervals from 2,000 bootstrap resamples of anchor draws, test texts, or writers

</div>

In WRIME, about 70% of the squared writer–reader discrepancy remains after removing the mean bias of each emotion, the bias of each writer, and the expected sampling error of the three readers. Most of the difference depends on the text.

## Experiment 1: Do prompts transfer a perspective?

<p class="experiment-label">Experiment 1</p>

We first measured the shift D between the reader-prompt and the writer-prompt k = 0 students, with no human labels involved.

<figure>
  <img src="/column-images/ssrn-writer-reader-disagreement/prompt-shift-1600.webp" alt="Prompt-induced shift of k=0 students" />
  <figcaption><strong>Figure 2.</strong> Prompt-induced shift D as a percentage of the reference gap G (dots = 3 seeds). Positive means toward the reader. The sign flips across seeds for both generators on crowd-enVent and for GPT-4o-mini with elicitation on WRIME; Qwen3-8B on WRIME moves consistently toward the writer.</figcaption>
</figure>

The shift ranged from −10% to +52% of G on crowd-enVent and from −38% to +11% on WRIME. Its sign changed across seeds for both generators on crowd-enVent and for GPT-4o-mini (elicitation) on WRIME. With Qwen3-8B on WRIME, the elicitation prompt moved the student consistently toward the writer, the opposite of the intended direction. The attribution prompt reduced this negative shift but did not reverse it. Since all conditions share the same label specifications, these shifts come from the instruction alone.

Generation prompts did not supply the missing perspective; it has to come from human labels.

## Experiment 2: What restores the population-level shift?

<p class="experiment-label">Experiment 2</p>

Next we adapted the students with k human labels and compared the population-level shift with the text-level alignment.

<figure>
  <img src="/column-images/ssrn-writer-reader-disagreement/shift-vs-alignment-1600.webp" alt="Population-level shift and text-level alignment at k=100" />
  <figcaption><strong>Figure 3.</strong> Population-level shift D/G (left) and text-level alignment A (right) at k = 100. Light grey bars are controls that do not use the pairing of texts and labels. The dotted line on the right is the alignment between two disjoint groups of readers, given as a scale. The marginal constant reads no text, so A is undefined for it.</figcaption>
</figure>

With 100 labels, D of the calibrated students was positive in every draw and reached 0.72–0.87 of G on crowd-enVent and 0.39–0.45 on WRIME across students, above the range of the generation prompts.

Most of this shift, however, did not need the pairing. With labels permuted across the anchor texts, the calibrated shift kept 75–90% of its paired value on crowd-enVent and 84–90% on WRIME. The marginal-constant predictor, which returns the anchors' label distribution for every text, reached D/G of 0.62 on crowd-enVent and 0.37 on WRIME, which is 71–95% of the calibrated shift. This is exactly what the identity for text-blind predictors predicts.

## Experiment 3: Can text-level differences be reproduced?

<p class="experiment-label">Experiment 3</p>

Paired labels did add text-level alignment beyond the label set, but alignment stayed low for every method.

On crowd-enVent, calibration had a mean A of 0.104–0.150 against at most 0.086 for the prior-ratio adjustment, and the 95% interval of the difference excluded zero for all four students. On WRIME, calibration exceeded its shuffled control by 0.022–0.029, but the prior-ratio adjustment reached a similar alignment without pairing.

Fine-tuning from the synthetic-data students carried more alignment than any label-only control. On crowd-enVent, A was 0.112–0.143 at k = 100 and 0.156–0.170 with 397 labels, above the reference models at 0.137. On WRIME it was 0.054–0.056 at k = 100 and 0.090–0.093 at k = 500. A shuffled-label control for fine-tuning confirmed the gain, with paired minus shuffled A of 0.040–0.047 and intervals excluding zero.

For scale, the label distributions of two disjoint reader groups aligned at 0.68 on crowd-enVent and 0.42 on WRIME.

In squared error, text-level differences were almost unexplained.

| Method (k = 100) | crowd-enVent M | crowd-enVent R²_text | WRIME M | WRIME R²_text |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Reference models | 0.23 | +0.010 | 0.32 | −0.030 |
| Calibration | 0.16 | +0.010 | 0.09 | −0.003 |
| Prior ratio | 0.16 | −0.001 | 0.13 | −0.011 |
| FT, anchors only | 0.32 | −0.040 | 0.11 | −0.005 |
| FT, synthetic + anchors | 0.59 | −0.195 | 0.32 | −0.068 |

R²_text stayed below 0.02 for every method and for the reference models, and fine-tuning made it negative. The M of fine-tuning (0.3–0.6) exceeded its A; an A of 0.055, for example, caps R²_text at 0.003. The predicted differences were overconfident. Shrinking the fine-tuned WRIME difference with development labels raised R²_text only to +0.003.

The best method also depended on the yardstick. On WRIME with 20 and 100 labels, calibration had the lowest pair NLL and fine-tuning from the synthetic-data student had the highest A, in all four cells. Across methods, label counts and draws, pair NLL and A had Spearman correlations between −0.51 and −0.80.

## Experiment 4: Does training on the difference help?

<p class="experiment-label">Experiment 4</p>

To check whether the problem is simply too few labels, we used all 30,000 WRIME training labels and trained on the difference itself.

<figure>
  <img src="/column-images/ssrn-writer-reader-disagreement/direct-supervision-1600.webp" alt="Direct difference supervision on WRIME" />
  <figcaption><strong>Figure 4.</strong> A (left) and R²_text (right) on WRIME for models trained on all labels. The top five rows read the text; the bottom two use the same writer's other test posts. "Writer mean, all" averages the observed difference over the writer's other posts without reading the text. * marks the model selected for A.</figcaption>
</figure>

All text models stayed between 0.10 and 0.13 in A. For the shared encoder with one head per perspective, with or without a squared loss on its predicted difference, the interval of the gain over separate models included zero. A regressor of the difference alone and a direction regressor trained with a cosine loss aligned less well than the shared encoder. After one scale chosen on development texts, the direction regressor reached R²_text = 0.011, close to its bound A² = 0.013. Its magnitude was nearly optimal; the direction was the limit.

Most of the remaining error was on the writer side. With separate models, the text-level error was 1.02 of the text-level variance, with a reader error of 0.24 and a writer error of 0.88. Averaging the observed difference over the writer's other posts reached A = 0.22 without reading the text, but differences between writers carry only 6% of the variance. Within writers, the shared encoder reached only 0.13.

## Experiment 5: Restoring the writer's emotion words

<p class="experiment-label">Experiment 5</p>

In crowd-enVent, explicit emotion words were masked in the texts shown to readers. We gave the writer model the unmasked text while keeping the reader model and its masked input fixed. Unmasking changed 270 of the 803 test texts.

<figure>
  <img src="/column-images/ssrn-writer-reader-disagreement/input-visibility-1600.webp" alt="Writer-side input visibility on crowd-enVent" />
  <figcaption><strong>Figure 5.</strong> A for different writer-model inputs. "+ keywords" is a simple rule that predicts the class named by a restored word and otherwise keeps the masked-text model.</figcaption>
</figure>

For the size-matched writer model, unmasking raised A from 0.130 to 0.208 (gain interval [+0.066, +0.090]). A writer model trained on the whole writer pool went from 0.23 to 0.39. But 266 of the 270 changed texts regained a word that names the writer's class.

A keyword rule that just predicts the class named by the restored word reached A = 0.37 with either writer model. That is 89% of the gain of the large model, and more than the size-matched model learned from unmasked texts. We read these gains as coming from **explicit class-name cues** rather than from learning how writers and readers judge differently.

## Experiment 6: A better predictor can make the difference worse

<p class="experiment-label">Experiment 6</p>

The text-level error splits as E_text = E_r + E_w − 2C, where E_r and E_w are the reader and writer errors and C is their covariance. If C falls by more than the writer error, the error of the difference grows.

<figure>
  <img src="/column-images/ssrn-writer-reader-disagreement/error-covariance-1600.webp" alt="Error split on crowd-enVent" />
  <figcaption><strong>Figure 6.</strong> Error split on crowd-enVent, as shares of the text-level variance (masked input, same reader model). The reader error E_r is 0.70 for all three trained rows and 0.88 for the mean prediction. Training on the whole writer pool lowers E_w, but C falls further and E_text rises to 1.28.</figcaption>
</figure>

The writer model trained on the whole writer pool lowered E_w from 1.42 to 0.91. Its error covariance with the reader model, however, fell from 0.57 to 0.16, and E_text rose from 0.99 to 1.28 (interval [+0.24, +0.35]), even though A rose by 0.10.

Shared training texts did not explain the high covariance of the smaller model. A writer model trained on the same number of writer-pool texts, disjoint from the reader model's texts, had a covariance of 0.564 against 0.567 with shared texts. The mean prediction, which gives each perspective its test mean, has a covariance of 0.72, equal to the covariance of the human writer and reader labels. Predictions near the mean inherit this covariance, which cancels in the difference; a more accurate writer model loses it. Training-set size and data composition remain confounded in this comparison.

## What we found and what remains open

The main findings are:

- Perspective instructions in generation prompts did not transfer a consistent perspective.
- A few human labels restore much of the population-level shift, but most of it can be reproduced from the label distribution alone. A large aggregate difference is not evidence that a model has learned on which texts writers and readers disagree.
- Text-level differences remained hard to reproduce even with all labels or direct training on the difference. Most remaining error is on the writer side, and improving one perspective can worsen the predicted difference.

From these results, the paper recommends that evaluations of perspective-aware models report the fit to each perspective, the average difference, and the direction and magnitude of text-level differences separately, together with label-only controls. It also argues that, because a writer's report depends on events and intentions the text does not show, recording writer context during generation and data collection is a natural next step.

There are limitations. We used two generators, one prompt format with three paraphrases per instruction, and one encoder student per corpus; stronger instructions or larger students may transfer more perspective. The hypotheses were formed after initial experiments on WRIME, and later analyses reuse the same data, so the four WRIME cells are not independent replications. The low alignment of the reference models may reflect limited training data, reader sampling error, and the single writer label, so no value is treated as an upper bound. The crowd-enVent reader pool has 397 texts, the writer-level checks used only the 10 WRIME test writers, and the masking study could not separate explicit from contextual cues because almost all changed texts regained a class name.

Next, we plan to collect writer context and repeated self-reports, develop models that treat the difference between perspectives as a training target, and apply the framework to other subjective tasks such as toxicity, sarcasm and stance.
